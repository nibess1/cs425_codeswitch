\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Many Singaporeans switch between English and Mandarin within a single sentence. In ``\zh{我明天要交} assignment \zh{啦}'' (``I have to submit my assignment tomorrow''), the sentence is mainly Mandarin, its \emph{matrix} language, and the English word ``assignment'' is \emph{embedded} in it. Such code-switched speech is hard for speech recognisers, and the embedded words are often the ones that matter most: a drug name, a deadline, a course code or a place.

Settings such as clinics, counselling and live captions for deaf users often cannot send audio to the cloud, so the recogniser has to run on the user's own device. The usual way to make a model small enough is \emph{quantization}: storing each weight in 8 or 4 bits instead of 16. Quantized models are normally judged by one overall error rate. In code-switched speech, however, embedded words are a small share of all words, so they can fail badly while the overall error rate barely moves.

Fine-tuning Whisper~\citep{radford2023whisper} on code-switched Mandarin--English speech improves accuracy~\citep{yang2024adapting,zhou2025csdialogue}, and \citet{ugan2025adapting} give switch points extra weight during training. Quantization of Whisper has been studied on monolingual speech~\citep{sohler2026quantizing,ferraz2024efficient}, where it hurts lower-resource languages more~\citep{ferraz2024efficient}. To our knowledge, no prior work measures how quantization changes errors on embedded words separately from errors on the rest of a mixed sentence, or asks whether embedded words fail because of the language they are in or because of the role they play in the sentence.

We study these questions with Whisper tiny, base and small on two corpora in which the embedded language is reversed: ASCEND~\citep{lovenia2022ascend}, mostly Mandarin with English embedded, and the Mandarin-speaker part of NSC Part~4~\citep{koh2019nsc}, mostly English with Mandarin embedded. We split every sentence into units (one Chinese character or one English word) and label each unit as embedded (\Emb), matrix next to a switch (\Ms) or matrix elsewhere (\Mi), so that every error can be charged to a role. We ask three research questions:
\textbf{RQ1}: where does Whisper fail on code-switched speech, and is it because of a unit's role or its language?
\textbf{RQ2}: do training and decoding methods aimed at embedded units reduce these errors at 16-bit?
\textbf{RQ3}: does quantization hurt embedded units more than matrix units, and can the damage be repaired?
Our hypotheses were that embedded units fail partly because of their role and not only their language (H1), that training and decoding aimed at embedded units lower their error without raising overall error (H2), and that quantization hurts embedded units more than matrix units, most in the smallest models (H3).

Our contributions and main findings are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A role-level error analysis} (RQ1). The same word has a higher error rate when it is embedded than when it belongs to the matrix language, even after controlling for language, word frequency and length. Fine-tuning removes this penalty for embedded Mandarin but not for embedded English. Contrary to our hypothesis, embedded words are rarely replaced by their translation; many are instead \emph{transliterated} into the other script (``deadline''$\rightarrow$``\zh{得来}''), although a small human audit suggests our automatic labels overcount transliterations.
  \item \textbf{Training and decoding methods for embedded units} (RQ2). A role-weighted loss combined with a sentence-level semantic loss lowers both overall and embedded-unit error over plain fine-tuning on both NSC test sets. Re-ranking candidate transcripts with a language-ID head helps embedded units in Mandarin-main speech but hurts English-main speech; running it only on clips where the head detects a language switch keeps the gain and removes the harm.
  \item \textbf{A measure of quantization fragility for embedded units and two fixes} (RQ3). 8-bit quantization is safe in every backend we test. At 4-bit, embedded-unit error rises 1.4 to 4.4 times as much as matrix error for the tiny and base models, while small is robust on the corpora; on our own phone recordings, NF4 hurts small's embedded words too. Keeping the most sensitive layers at 16-bit, or quantization-aware fine-tuning with QLoRA~\citep{dettmers2023qlora}, recovers about half to nine-tenths of the embedded-unit loss on most affected slices, but the QLoRA gain does not carry over to the 4-bit format used on device.
\end{itemize}

Our best system, fine-tuned Whisper small with both losses, lowers the overall error on the NSC test set from 55.1\% to 20.6\% and the embedded-unit error from 76.7\% to 21.3\% compared with the off-the-shelf model. Stored in 8-bit with whisper.cpp~\citep{whispercpp}, it is a 252\,MB file that runs at 0.57 times real time on a laptop CPU. Code, scoring tools and all result tables are at \url{https://github.com/nibess1/cs425_codeswitch}.
